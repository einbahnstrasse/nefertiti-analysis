Three scales of attention

What changes when a melody returns but musicians change how they play together? In the Miles Davis Quintet’s 1968 recording of “Nefertiti,” recurring statements of Wayne Shorter’s sixteen-bar composition coexist with changes in articulation, rhythmic grouping, instrumental participation, and foreground activity. An account of the composition’s melody and harmony helps explain its continuity, but this is not the whole musical experience. Understanding the performance, and therefore what the compsition was designed to facilitate, also requires examining how improvising musicians make it possible to introduce, answer, transform, and leave space for one another’s ideas.

Ingrid Monson makes this analytical need explicit in Saying Something: Jazz Improvisation and Interaction. She proposes a communicative perspective for exploring the “relatively neglected—yet aesthetically central—interactive aspects of jazz improvisation.”1 Here, instrumental roles are interdependent and flexible: musicians make choices “in relationship to what everyone else is doing.”2 Interaction, therefore, belongs within the scope of any meaningful or convincing analysis of improvised music. It can shape a phrase, redistribute instrumental roles, and contribute to the direction of an entire performance.

Monson demonstrates this approach at different scales. Her analysis of Geri Allen and Ralph Peterson on “Princess” traces rhythmic interventions, interruptions, and subsequent responses between piano and drums.3 Her extended analysis of “Bass-ment Blues” examines how exchanges, recovery, and stylistic references contribute to intensification across successive choruses.4 These examples suggest an approach to “Nefertiti” that connects individual gestures with developments across choruses and with changes in the performance as a whole.

David Borgo supplies a complementary account of recursive interaction. In Sync or Swarm, his discussion developed with Joseph Goguen describes music making and listening as coupled processes: “the current state of each acts as the control of the other.”5 Musicians hear the ensemble sound, act in relation to it, and thereby change the sound available for subsequent listening and action — and the piece is updated as it is unfolding in realtime. A response can also change how an earlier gesture is understood.6 In our analysis, a feedback connection identifies a relationship of this kind. The mere return of the sixteen-bar form establishes recurrence, but identifying interpersonal recursion requires evidence of uptake, transformation, or adjustment.

Two further perspectives clarify what this approach attends to. Paul Berliner describes the solo as one strand within a texture whose accompanying parts are themselves commonly improvised.7 George E. Lewis emphasizes how personal sound communicates meanings beyond pitches and intervals.8 Together, these perspectives encourage attention to the rhythmic and timbral site specificity of the quintet’s exchanges — on this particular take, on this day at Columbia Records’ 30th Street Studio in 1967.

The interaction-decision graphs below offer three interconnected scales of attention:

  • Performance: How do ensemble participation, synchronization, and instrumental prominence change across this recording as a whole? This view connects larger statements across the performance.
  • Passage: How do exchanges develop across phrases and choruses? This view examines recurring opportunities for intervention, following changes that may persist into subsequent choruses.
  • Local exchange: What does one musician introduce, what follows, and what is transformed? This view examines specific instances at a “local” level — individually asserted ideas at specific moments.

These are treated as complementary zoom levels rather than a fixed hierarchy of musical prominance, such as those proposed by Schenkerian analyses. A local exchange may remain local or contribute to a wider change; the graphs below flexibly allow those relationships to be investigated. All timestamps refer to the assigned YouTube recording.9 Listening observations and literature accounts are attributed separately, and response relationships remain distinguishable from the reported sequence of events.
